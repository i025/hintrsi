# Pre-Test mechanism comparison

## Scope and comparability

This is a semantic comparison of the supplied libraries and V2 analyses, not an evaluation of a student. No Selection or Test tasks or results were supplied. K1 and K2 V1 used the same fixed raw D01–D08 cohort. V2 instead draws on each arm’s independent on-policy traces and previous arm memory. Differences in V2 content therefore cannot isolate an effect of functional-state alignment.

The delivered libraries also differ in length and coverage: K1 V2 contains 619 words in four units. K2 V2 was 707 words raw, exceeded the shared 700-word cap, and the pre-saved atomic rule delivered 505 words in three units. The K2 analysis is evidence about the update process, not extra guidance delivered to the student. In particular, its separate unit on failures and verification boundaries must not be restored as a fourth delivered unit.

## Shared constraints and the K0 prior

Across the arms, the recurring implementation-independent idea is to identify what a contractually relevant consumer needs across a boundary, then inspect the actual values, identities, ordering, errors, or representation it receives. This is conditional on the task, not a requirement to use a particular state model. K1 V1’s **“Trace behavior across boundaries”** and K2 V1’s **“Inspect state across transitions”** both direct attention beyond a local invocation; K2 is more explicitly organized around state before and after a transition. D02 events 30–33, 59–61 and D05 events 49–57, 95–100 support the boundary concern, with the important limit that D05’s later saved-context change was not separately probed.

Other common constraints include checking meaningful outputs rather than call counts, respecting identity and error semantics, separating representation from meaning, and bounding verification claims. K1 V1’s **“Verify transformations beyond an example”** and K2 V1’s **“Preserve meaning when changing representation”** both draw on D08 events 53–63 and 78–79: focused passes coexisted with formal failures of unknown cause. They emphasize different aspects—independent properties versus meaningful units of equivalence—but neither establishes universal preservation.

Much of this advice was already plausible from the teacher prior. K0 V1 **“Model stateful control flow and failure propagation”** calls for lifecycle tracing, identifier ownership, caller responses and distinct failure channels; **“Treat persisted schemas as an inference contract”** covers ordering, downstream application and conflicting sources. K0 V1 **“Use syntax structure without losing source fidelity”** and **“Translate contracts into observable checks”** likewise anticipate representation-aware checks and bounded verification. K0 V2 adds explicit stable identity, partial recovery and preservation of accepted information in **“Model lifecycle, recovery, and recursive execution,”** and separate field outcomes and non-mutating refinement in **“Preserve boundaries in structured data and source text.”** These are proposals from descriptions and prior knowledge, not observations of a student or proof of transfer.

## What K2 alignment adds—and what it does not

K2 V2’s **“Preserve usable state during continuation”** explicitly aligns Cattrs partial refinement with the older continuation and schema examples: the next consumer may need both prior usable state and new input, while successful and failed components may need different treatment. The analysis grounds that analogy in Cattrs / `main_agent` / 41–42, 63–64 and D05 events 49–57, 95–100 and D06 events 25–26, 27–54. The implementations are complementary, not identical. K1 V2’s chronological analysis describes the Cattrs probes and its **“Verify transformation properties independently”** also tells the reader to inspect retained successful information. What is distinctive in K2 is the explicit cross-task functional alignment of these examples, not the mere idea of retaining prior work.

K2 V2 **“Preserve meaningful identity across transitions”** makes a second explicit alignment: D05’s teacher-prompted request/response identifier checks (events 84–85, 108–109) and Snapshot’s identity-field change are related by consumer-relevant identity rather than by a shared implementation. Snapshot / `main_agent` / 33–45 supports a change and selected passing probe, but not a demonstrated hooked running-to-terminated identity match. K1 V2 **“Trace behavior across boundaries and time”** also names identity and lifecycle transitions; its chronological analysis discusses retained snapshot categories. K2’s sharper name-versus-identity rule is a delivered emphasis, but stable identity was already explicit in K0 V2. It is not novel knowledge attributable to alignment alone.

K2 V2 **“Reconcile representations without weakening contracts”** aligns typed-field repair with schema and formatting boundaries. Cattrs / `main_agent` / 43–45, 47–62, 65–84 shows a wrapper repair and a generic repair that initially broke, then passed, the earlier basic reproduction. D06 events 68–70 were partly truncated; D08 events 53–63, 78–79 had focused passes alongside unexplained formal failures. The specific observed regression-and-retest sequence supports K2’s instruction to rerun earlier reproductions after later edits. K1 V2 **“Reproduce, probe, and retest repairs”** also directs a same-probe rerun, supported by Cattrs events 74–79 and 82–89; K1’s nested-state edit had no visible same-probe rerun. Thus the functional grouping differs, but retesting is not uniquely delivered by K2.

The K2 analysis additionally aligns worker versus unknown-target feedback, field versus global validation, and missing-snapshot responses as distinct error contracts (D05 events 84–85; Cattrs / `main_agent` / 85–86; Snapshot / `main_agent` / 43–46). That analysis does **not** make its separate failure-reporting unit delivered. K2’s three delivered units retain narrower cautions about task-specific contracts, validation and bounded focused passes. K1 V2, by contrast, actually delivers the separate **“Interpret and report verification precisely”** unit, including unfinished runs, unavailable tools, baselines and unidentified formal failures.

## Representation versus instruction, and applicability

Some differences are primarily organization or clearer wording. K1 generally presents chronological investigation and verification practices; K2 groups examples by inferred state-transition function. Both tell a reader to inspect boundaries and limit conclusions. Calling the same concern “retained state” rather than “downstream use,” or separating K2’s state and identity into two units, does not by itself establish additional knowledge. The actual delivered emphasis does differ: K1 V2 explicitly asks for reporting incomplete verification and distinguishes an absent API from a demonstrated defect; K2 V2 explicitly warns against using a convenient label as stable identity and against weakening unrelated validation during reconciliation. These are differences in instructions as written, not evidence that either student would behave differently.

Applicability can conflict with a literal reading of the guidance. Retaining old state is wrong when the contract requires discarding it; repairing only failed components is inappropriate when a whole result must be recomputed. Stable cross-transition identity is inappropriate if each representation is intentionally a new entity. Likewise, equivalence, idempotence, alias reconciliation, baselines and pre-change reproductions are not universal requirements: a transformation may intentionally change meaning, an API may not yet exist, or a safe baseline may be unavailable. K0 and both arms warn against imposing unspecified policies. Exhaustive lifecycle maps, broad suites, extra conversions or repeated probes can waste effort or introduce unnecessary changes when a task is local or execution is prohibited.

Direct student actions that would support adoption include identifying the applicable contract, inspecting both sides of a relevant boundary, checking retained and newly produced values, testing identity distinctions where required, rerunning a motivating probe after a repair, and accurately reporting what completed. A visible rationale connecting such actions to a supplied unit would strengthen evidence of use. Even then, the actions could also follow from task instructions, existing expertise, repository conventions or the K0 prior; they would not prove that alignment caused them.

## Bounded pre-Test hypotheses

On suitable stateful tasks, delivered K2 V2 may prompt more explicit checks of consumer-relevant continuity, selective retention and identity across transitions. On tasks with incomplete verification or a missing API, delivered K1 V2 may prompt more explicit qualification and same-probe repair reporting. Either library may encourage boundary inspection already plausible from K0. These are hypotheses about possible behavior under applicable contracts, not predicted scores or outcomes, and the independent V2 traces and unequal delivered coverage prevent a causal comparison of alignment alone.
